\small
\textbf{Abstract.} We study the cross-sectional and intertemporal association between
university intellectual-property policy communication and technology-transfer activity
using a novel text-based measure developed in \citet{sharma2026pcsi}. The Policy Communication
Stance Index (\PCSI{}) encodes the tone, clarity, and legal load of 150 U.S.\ research
university IP policy documents over 1991--2023 using sentence-level BERT embeddings,
producing approximately 87,160 scored sentences across 2,564 institution-year observations.
Merging \PCSI{} with AUTM survey data, we document that universities with more
supportive and structured IP-policy communication are associated with higher values of
the log application-to-disclosure index---a measure of institutional triage efficiency
constructed as $\ln(1+\text{patent applications}) - \ln(1+\text{invention disclosures})$.
The lag-1 conditional association is $\hat\beta \approx 1.00$ (SE$\approx$0.50,
raw $p\approx.047$); after Benjamini-Hochberg correction across eight outcomes, the
adjusted $p$-value rises to 0.374, and the result does not survive conventional
multiple-testing thresholds. A lag-2 association ($\hat\beta \approx 1.34$, $p\approx.014$)
is more robust but remains associational. A falsification test finds that \PCSI{} also
predicts future research expenditure ($\hat\beta\approx+0.44$, $p\approx.084$), indicating
that reverse causation or common time-varying confounders cannot be ruled out. An
exploratory event-study diagnostic, demoted to an appendix, recovers only three
high-confidence policy-revision events with verified effective dates; all three show
pre-revision differences that preclude credible parallel-trends inference. We interpret
these patterns as descriptive evidence consistent with a governance-mediation mechanism
but emphasize that the observational design does not establish that policy communication
itself causes the observed associations.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

University intellectual property policies are among the most consequential governance
documents in the research enterprise. They determine how faculty inventions are disclosed,
evaluated, and commercialized; they signal institutional priorities to inventors, licensees,
and prospective employees; and they shape the incentive environment within which
technology-transfer offices (TTOs) operate. Despite a large empirical literature on
university patenting and licensing \citep{henderson1998, mowery2001, dechenaux2011},
relatively little is known about how the \emph{communication stance} of these
policies---their tone, clarity, and legal complexity---correlates with technology-transfer
outcomes.

This paper provides descriptive evidence on that question. Our contribution is explicitly
measurement- and association-based: we do not claim to estimate causal effects of policy
communication, and we describe in detail the identification limitations that prevent such
claims. Instead, we document four things. First, we introduce and validate the
\PCSI{} (Policy Communication Stance Index), a sentence-level BERT-based measure of
IP policy communication applied to approximately 87,160 sentences from 150 U.S.\ research
universities over 1991--2023. Second, we document a systematic institutional regularity:
86\% of institution-year \PCSI{} observations fall below the 0.5 neutral midpoint,
indicating that U.S.\ university IP policies are predominantly restrictive in
communicative stance---a cross-sectional fact that has not been previously quantified.
Third, we document that \PCSI{} is more strongly and consistently associated with the
log application-to-disclosure index (a measure of TTO triage efficiency) than with
patent volume per se---an outcome-specificity result with implications for how researchers
model the governance-to-commercialization pathway. Fourth, we are explicit about the
identification limits: the falsification test, the absence of credible policy-revision
events for parallel-trends inference, and the multiple-testing fragility of the primary
association.

\textbf{The central finding is this:} Universities with more supportive and structured
IP-policy communication are associated with stronger medium-term disclosure-to-patent
conversion, particularly at two-year lags. The evidence is consistent with a TTO-mediated
governance mechanism---more structured and supportive policies may reduce ambiguity in
disclosure handling, standardize TTO procedures, and clarify inventor expectations---but
the observational design does not establish that policy communication itself causes the
observed improvement.

\section{Relationship to the Companion Measurement Paper}
\label{sec:paper1}
% ============================================================

This paper is the empirical companion to \citet{sharma2026pcsi}, which develops and
validates the \PCSI{} measure. All measurement details---sentence corpus construction,
BERT fine-tuning, dimensionality reduction into tone, clarity, and legal-load sub-indices,
and validation exercises including inter-rater reliability and convergent validity---are
reported there and are not repeated here. We take the \PCSI{} scores as given inputs.

One reconciliation item: \citet{sharma2026pcsi} reports approximately 87,321 scored
sentences; after removing three BYU parsing artifacts (institution-years 2021--2023 with
implausibly long mean sentence length $\approx$4,006 words) and reconciling one duplicate
entry, the working corpus used in this paper contains approximately 87,160 sentences.
This difference does not affect any institution-level aggregates materially.

\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% ============================================================

Why might the communication stance of an IP policy be associated with technology-transfer
outcomes? We sketch a simple mechanism without claiming it is identified.

Universities operate under a regime of institutional ambiguity: the Bayh-Dole Act
\citep{mowery2001} gave universities ownership of federally funded inventions but left
the internal governance of disclosure, evaluation, and commercialization almost entirely
to institutional discretion. TTOs consequently vary enormously in policy and practice
\citep{siegel2003, siegel2004, thursby2002}. Within this variance, we conjecture that the
communication stance of the governing IP policy document may be associated with TTO
behavior along two channels.

The first channel is \emph{inventor clarity}. A policy document that is written in
accessible, supportive language with low legal load reduces the transaction cost of
disclosure for faculty inventors \citep{jensenthursby2001, thursbythursby2002}. If
faculty face uncertainty about what constitutes a disclosable invention, or about whether
the TTO is likely to be a cooperative partner, they may under-disclose or delay disclosure.
A more supportive policy may reduce that uncertainty.

The second channel is \emph{TTO governance}. IP policy documents are not only external
signals to inventors; they also constitute internal governance rules for TTO staff.
A more structured and specific policy---reflected in higher clarity and lower legal
load---may standardize evaluation procedures, reduce ad hoc decision-making, and improve
the consistency of the triage process that converts disclosures into patent applications.
This channel predicts an effect specifically on the application-to-disclosure ratio, which
is the outcome we find to be most consistently associated with \PCSI{}.

We do not have data to directly test either channel. The TTO-mediated mechanism is offered
as a plausible interpretation, not as an established result.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Technology transfer from universities.}
A substantial literature studies the determinants of university patenting and licensing
following the Bayh-Dole Act \citep{mowery2001, henderson1998, sampat2006}. \citet{dechenaux2011}
survey the empirical literature and conclude that TTO resources, institutional prestige,
and licensing strategy are the most robust predictors of licensing income and startups;
raw patent counts are less informative. \citet{thursby2002} document wide heterogeneity
in TTO efficiency across institutions. \citet{siegel2003, siegel2004} emphasize the role
of organizational factors---staffing norms, incentive structures---that are independent
of research quality. Our paper contributes to this strand by introducing a communication
measure that may capture an underexplored governance dimension.

\paragraph{Text-based measurement in economics.}
The application of natural language processing to governance and policy documents has
grown substantially \citep{gentzkow2019, loughranmcdonald2011, gentzkowkellytaddy2019}.
\citet{loughranmcdonald2011} demonstrate that standard sentiment lexicons are poorly
calibrated for legal and financial documents, motivating domain-specific approaches.
\citet{gentzkowkellytaddy2019} develop a general framework for text-as-data in economics
emphasizing measurement validity and the distinction between descriptive and causal
claims. Our measure follows their guidance in treating the text scores as
observational proxies, not experimental treatments.

\paragraph{Policy communication and institutional behavior.}
\citet{dimaggio1983} and \citet{meyer1977} establish the isomorphism logic under which
organizational documents carry legitimacy functions independent of their operational
content. \citet{suchman1995} develops the legitimacy theory most directly applicable to
university technology policy. \citet{moynihan2015} reviews the governance-communication
literature in public administration. Our paper is the first, to our knowledge, to apply
this framework to university IP policy documents in a panel with quantitative outcomes.

\paragraph{Event studies and parallel trends in education and innovation contexts.}
Recent methodological advances have substantially raised the bar for event-study
identification \citep{callaway2021, sunabr2021, goodmanbacon2021, roth2022,
rambachanroth2023, borusyakjaravel2024}. \citet{dechaisemartin2020} and
\citet{dechaisemartin2023} survey heterogeneous treatment effect estimators for staggered
adoption designs. \citet{rothsantanna2023} propose sensitivity analysis for parallel
trends violations. We rely on these papers to justify our decision to demote the event
study to an appendix: when a design cannot pass the high-confidence event verification
test and when pre-revision differences are visible in the data, event-study coefficients
should be treated as descriptive summaries, not causal estimates.

\paragraph{Positioning and contribution.}
This paper contributes to the literature on the non-resource determinants of TTO
performance. We are neither the first to study IP policy governance \citep{coase1960,
williamson1985, herd2019} nor the first to measure policy documents quantitatively
\citep{loughranmcdonald2011, ashchenaidu2022, devlin2019}. Our specific contribution
is the intersection: a validated text-based measure of IP policy communication applied
to a large panel, with careful attention to what the variation identifies and what it
does not. We interpret our findings as opening a measurement agenda rather than settling
a causal question.

\subsection{Sample construction}

Table~\ref{tab:sampleflow} summarizes the sample construction. We begin with 3,507
institution-year observations from 253 universities in the AUTM licensing survey,
covering 1991--2023. Matching to our \PCSI{} panel (which covers 150 universities with
at least one IP policy document in the corpus) yields 2,564 observations across 150
institutions. Lagging \PCSI{} by one year and dropping the first year for each institution
reduces the sample to 2,182 observations; adding controls further reduces it to 2,115.
This is our baseline regression sample.

\subsection{AUTM outcomes}

We use annual AUTM survey data on seven technology-transfer outcomes: new patent
applications, total patent applications, patents issued, invention disclosures, licenses
executed, startups formed, and licensing income. All count outcomes are transformed as
$\ln(1 + y)$ to accommodate zeros. The primary outcome in our analysis is the
\textit{log application-to-disclosure index}, constructed as:
\begin{equation}
  A_{it} = \ln(1 + \text{PatApps}_{it}) - \ln(1 + \text{Disclosures}_{it})
  \label{eq:convrate}
\end{equation}
This is an institution-year accounting index that measures the ratio of patent
application activity to disclosure activity. It is \emph{not} a tracked cohort
conversion rate: disclosures in year $t$ may generate patent applications in year
$t$, $t+1$, or $t+2$, and the index does not attempt to match cohorts. Higher values
indicate that an institution is applying for patents at a higher rate relative to
incoming disclosures, which we interpret as a triage-efficiency signal from the TTO.

Institutional controls include the log of total research expenditure (lagged one year),
the log of licensing FTEs (lagged one year), and the inventor royalty share. TLO age
is collinear with institution and year fixed effects and is omitted.

\subsection{Policy Communication Stance Index}

The \PCSI{} is described in detail in Paper~1. Briefly, it is computed from
sentence-level BERT scores aggregated to the institution-year level. It ranges from
approximately 0.3 to 0.7 in our sample, with higher values indicating more supportive
and clearer language. A key feature of the data is that 89\% of matched \PCSI{}
observations are carried forward from the most recently available policy document
rather than freshly observed in that calendar year; the median policy age at
observation is 6 years (mean 7.8 years). This implies that the effective variation
used in our regressions is primarily cross-sectional, not within-institution
over time, a limitation we discuss further in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}
% ============================================================

Our baseline specification is a two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) panel regression:
\begin{equation}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta \cdot \PCSI{}_{i,t-1} + \mathbf{X}_{i,t-1}'\gamma + \varepsilon_{it}
  \label{eq:baseline}
\end{equation}
where $Y_{it}$ is a technology-transfer outcome for institution $i$ in year $t$,
$\alpha_i$ are institution fixed effects, $\lambda_t$ are year fixed effects,
$\PCSI{}_{i,t-1}$ is the lagged \PCSI{} measure, and $\mathbf{X}_{i,t-1}$ is a
vector of lagged controls. Standard errors are clustered at the institution level
with the \citet{cameron2015} $G-1$ degrees-of-freedom correction.

We emphasize that $\hat\beta$ identifies the conditional association between
\PCSI{} and outcomes within the TWFE structure, not a causal effect. The institution
fixed effects absorb time-invariant differences across universities; the year fixed
effects absorb common temporal shocks. What remains is the within-institution
correlation between \PCSI{} deviations and outcome deviations, conditional on controls.
Because 89\% of \PCSI{} observations are carried forward (Section~\ref{sec:data}),
the within-institution variation is largely driven by the 37 policy-revision events
rather than smooth annual updates. This means our estimates are effectively a
weighted average of: (a)~the within-revision contrast for the 30 institutions with
policy changes, and (b)~pure cross-sectional variation for the remaining 120 institutions.

We estimate equation~(\ref{eq:baseline}) for each of eight outcomes: the seven AUTM
outcomes and the log application-to-disclosure index. We report raw $p$-values and
Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted $p$-values \citep{bakerlarkerwang2022} for the full
set of outcomes. The BH adjustment is essential context for interpreting individual
results: as we show in Section~\ref{sec:results}, the primary finding does not survive
conventional multiple-testing correction.

\subsection{Circular-shift placebo}

To assess whether the lag-1 patent-application association ($\hat\beta=0.616$) is
unusual relative to randomly permuted \PCSI{} assignments, we conduct a circular-shift
placebo test: we randomly rotate each institution's \PCSI{} time series by a random
number of periods (preserving the autocorrelation structure within institution) and
re-estimate the baseline regression 2,000 times. The placebo $p$-value---the fraction
of permutations generating a coefficient at least as large in absolute value as the
observed---is 0.193. The observed coefficient is not unusual under this permutation
distribution, consistent with the absence of statistical significance for patent volumes.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
% ============================================================

Appendix Table~B1 reports a robustness suite for both log new patent applications and
the log application-to-disclosure index. For the application-to-disclosure index, the
conditional association is robust to: winsorizing at the 1st/99th percentiles
($\hat\beta=1.224$, $p=0.038$); restricting to the balanced panel with at least 15
observations ($\hat\beta=1.046$, $p=0.066$); and restricting to R1 universities
($\hat\beta=1.166$, $p=0.053$). The lag-2 association is stronger ($\hat\beta=1.343$,
$p=0.014$) and constitutes the most statistically robust result in the paper.

For log new patent applications, with HC3 standard errors the coefficient reaches
nominal significance ($\hat\beta=0.616$, SE$=0.308$, $p=0.046$), but this reflects
the more optimistic variance estimator rather than stronger identification.

A leave-one-out analysis (dropping one institution at a time) yields coefficients ranging
from $+0.340$ to $+0.874$ for log patent applications, with 100\% positive, indicating
the association is not driven by any single institution.

The falsification test---regressing future (year $t+1$) log research expenditure on
current \PCSI{}---yields $\hat\beta=+0.440$ ($p=0.084$). This is a primary identification
concern: if \PCSI{} predicts a resource input, the association with outputs may partly
reflect common time-varying factors or reverse causation rather than a policy-communication
channel. We discuss this further in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

% ============================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% ============================================================

We enumerate the main limitations of this study. Readers should weigh these against
the reported findings.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Observational design, no causal identification.} This study establishes
conditional associations, not causal effects. We have no instrument for \PCSI{} and no
credible natural experiment. The TWFE structure absorbs institution and year heterogeneity
but does not eliminate confounding from time-varying, institution-specific factors.

\item \textbf{Falsification concern.} The most serious identification concern is that
\PCSI{} predicts future research expenditure ($\hat\beta\approx+0.440$, $p\approx.084$).
This pattern is consistent with reverse causation (more active institutions revise
policies more supportively) or with a common factor (resource growth simultaneously
drives policy revision and patent activity). We cannot distinguish these alternatives
with the available data.

\item \textbf{Multiple-testing fragility.} The primary finding (lag-1 log
application-to-disclosure index, $p=0.047$) does not survive BH correction
(BH-adjusted $p=0.374$). The statistical case for the primary finding rests on the
lag-2 result ($p=0.014$) and robustness checks, not on the raw lag-1 $p$-value.

\item \textbf{Carried-forward observations.} 89\% of \PCSI{} observations are
carried forward from the most recently available policy document. The effective
within-institution variation is therefore sparse and largely concentrated in the
37 policy-revision years. This means the regression is not exploiting continuous
policy updates but rather a small number of discrete changes and cross-sectional
differences.

\item \textbf{Policy age and staleness.} The mean policy age at observation is 7.8
years (median 6). Many institutions in the sample had not revised their policies
in over a decade. Whether a 10-year-old policy document meaningfully reflects
current TTO governance is uncertain.

\item \textbf{Mechanical index properties.} The log application-to-disclosure index
is a difference in log transforms of two AUTM survey items. Changes in the index
may reflect disclosure under-reporting, application over-filing, or changes in
disclosure thresholds rather than TTO triage quality. We cannot separate these
mechanically.

\item \textbf{Sentence corpus discrepancy.} Paper~1 reports approximately 87,321
scored sentences; our working corpus contains approximately 87,160 sentences after
removing three BYU parsing artifacts and reconciling one duplicate entry. We
document this explicitly to ensure replication transparency.

\item \textbf{No parallel trends for event study.} A high-confidence audit of
policy-revision events recovers only 3 verified events (Louisiana State University,
Oklahoma State University, University of New Orleans), all showing pre-revision
differences in outcomes. The event-study diagnostic in Appendix~E should be
interpreted as a descriptive summary, not a causal estimate.

\item \textbf{AUTM sample selection.} AUTM survey participation is voluntary. If
institutions with more active TTOs are more likely to participate, and if
policy-communication quality is correlated with TTO activity, our estimates may
not generalize to the full population of U.S.\ research universities.

\item \textbf{Absence of faculty-level data.} The governance mechanism we conjecture
operates through faculty disclosure behavior. We have no faculty-level data and
cannot test the mechanism directly.

\item \textbf{Policy document authenticity.} Some institutions may maintain
aspirational or ceremonial policy documents that do not reflect actual TTO practice
\citep{dimaggio1983, meyer1977}. \PCSI{} measures the text of the document,
not its operational implementation.

\item \textbf{Confounding from policy revision timing.} Institutions may revise
policies precisely when they are experiencing TTO transitions, new leadership,
or strategic shifts. If policy revision is endogenous to the same dynamics that
drive outcome changes, the within-institution estimates are contaminated.

\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================
\section{Exploratory Policy-Revision Diagnostic}
\label{sec:eventdiag}
% ============================================================

Appendix~E presents an exploratory analysis of the 37 policy-revision years identified
in our \PCSI{} data ($|\Delta\PCSI{}|>0.05$, across 30 institutions). This analysis
is explicitly \emph{not} an event study in the causal-identification sense. A
high-confidence audit recovers only 3 events with independently verified effective
dates (Louisiana State University, Oklahoma State University, and the University of
New Orleans), and all three institutions show pre-revision differences in outcomes
that violate the parallel-trends assumption required for causal interpretation
\citep{callaway2021, roth2022, rambachanroth2023}.

We include the diagnostic because it provides descriptive evidence on the
post-revision trajectory of outcomes, and because the positive targeted DiD
coefficient ($\hat\beta\approx+0.458$, $p=0.003$) is consistent with the
direction of the panel results. We do not describe this as corroborating the
main finding; a result that relies on an invalid identifying assumption is not
corroboration.

% ============================================================
\section{Policy Implications}
\label{sec:policy}
% ============================================================

The results in this paper are associational and the identification concerns are serious.
We therefore offer modest and conditional implications.

The cross-sectional regularity---86\% of U.S.\ university IP policies score below the
0.5 communicative-stance neutral point---suggests that most universities maintain
policies that are predominantly restrictive in tone, heavy in legal load, or unclear
in guidance to potential inventors. Whether this reflects deliberate strategy, legal
risk management, institutional inertia, or simply the drafting conventions of legal
counsel is an open question. The pattern is consistent with the isomorphism account
of \citet{dimaggio1983} and \citet{meyer1977}: policies may be ceremonially isomorphic
without being functionally distinct.

The association between more supportive \PCSI{} and the log application-to-disclosure
index---if it is not entirely spurious---is consistent with the view that policy
communication quality affects TTO triage efficiency more than research volume.
Administrators considering policy revisions should be cautious about expecting
large effects on patent counts, but may find the triage-efficiency interpretation
more plausible for internal process improvements. However, we emphasize that observational
data cannot support a recommendation to revise policy communication in expectation
of improved outcomes; the effect size and the direction of causality remain uncertain.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We have studied the association between university IP policy communication stance and
technology-transfer outcomes using a text-based panel that merges a novel BERT-based
measure with three decades of AUTM survey data. The main findings are: (i)~U.S.\ research
universities predominantly communicate their IP policies in a restrictive tone (86\% of
observations below the 0.5 neutral midpoint); (ii)~\PCSI{} is most consistently
associated with the log application-to-disclosure index, not with patent volume,
suggesting an outcome-specificity pattern with implications for the governance-to-commercialization
model; and (iii)~the primary lag-1 association, while nominally significant at the 5\%
level, does not survive Benjamini-Hochberg multiple-testing correction (adjusted
$p=0.374$), while the lag-2 association ($p=0.014$) is more robust.

We are explicit that these are descriptive panel associations. The falsification test
(PCSI predicts future research expenditure, $p=0.084$), the absence of credible
policy-revision events for parallel-trends inference, and the high fraction of
carried-forward observations all counsel against causal interpretation. The contribution
of this paper is measurement---introducing and applying a validated text-based instrument
to a question that has lacked quantitative indicators---and association-documentation.
The causal question of whether changing IP policy communication improves technology
transfer remains open.

\section{Exploratory Policy-Revision Diagnostic}
\label{app:eventdiag}

\textbf{Important caveat.} This appendix presents a descriptive analysis of
outcome trajectories around policy-revision events identified in our \PCSI{} data.
It is \emph{not} causal identification. The parallel-trends assumption is not
credible in this setting: a high-confidence audit recovers only three AUTM-linked
revision events with verified effective dates (Louisiana State University, Oklahoma
State University, and the University of New Orleans), and these institutions show
substantial pre-revision differences in outcomes relative to the comparison group.
The estimates below should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of post-revision
trajectories, not as causal effects of policy revision.

We define policy-revision events as institution-years in which $|\Delta\PCSI{}|>0.05$
(37 events, 30 institutions). The targeted DiD specification uses event-time windows
$e_t \in \{-4,\ldots,+4\}$ relative to the revision year, with comparison groups
drawn from non-revising institutions in the same year. Following the notation of
\citet{callaway2021} and \citet{sunabr2021}, we estimate:

\begin{equation}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \sum_{k \neq -1} \delta_k \cdot \mathbf{1}[e_{it}=k] + \varepsilon_{it}
\end{equation}

Figure~\ref{fig:pretrend} shows the event-study estimates. Pre-revision coefficients
are positive and increasing for several periods before the revision year,
indicating that revising institutions were already on a different trajectory. The
post-revision coefficients continue to rise, but the pre-trend pattern means the
post-revision estimates conflate a genuine post-revision change with the pre-existing
trend. The targeted DiD estimate for event-times $\{+2,+3\}$ versus $\{-4,-3\}$
is $\hat\delta = +0.458$ (SE $= 0.143$, $p = 0.003$), but this estimate should not
be interpreted causally given the pre-trend evidence.
