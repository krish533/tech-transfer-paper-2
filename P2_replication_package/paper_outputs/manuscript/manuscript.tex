\textbf{Abstract.}
We study how the linguistic character of university intellectual property (IP) policies
relates to downstream technology transfer performance.
Using a BERT-based sentence classifier applied to policy documents from 150
research universities (1944--2025), we construct a
\emph{Policy Communication Stance Index} (\PCSI{}) that captures the supportiveness,
clarity, and legal load of each institution's IP policy language.
Merging this measure with AUTM survey data (253 institutions, 1991--2023),
we estimate two-way fixed-effects models that hold constant all time-invariant
institutional characteristics and common time trends.
We find that a one-unit increase in lagged \PCSI{} predicts a
\textbf{one-log-point increase in the conversion rate from invention
disclosures to patent applications} (SE = 0.500, $p = 0.047$),
with the effect strengthening at a two-year lag ($\beta = 1.343$, $p = 0.014$).
No comparable relationship emerges for patent volumes.
A direction-split event study corroborates this pattern: institutions
revising policies in a more supportive direction show significantly higher
post-revision conversion rates than institutions revising toward restriction,
with a targeted difference-in-differences estimate of $+0.458$ log points
($p = 0.003$).
Effects concentrate among R1 and non-land-grant institutions.
The evidence is consistent with a model in which policy clarity reduces
inventor transaction costs at the disclosure--patent triage stage.
We discuss important identification limitations and do not claim causality.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The commercialization of academic research depends not only on the quantity of
scientific output, but also on the institutional infrastructure that converts
scientific discoveries into economically valuable innovations.
Central to this infrastructure are a university's intellectual property (IP)
policies --- formal governance documents that define the rights and obligations
of researchers, institutions, and private partners when faculty generate
potentially patentable inventions.

Since the Bayh-Dole Act of 1980 granted universities title to federally funded
inventions, technology transfer has become a significant strategic and financial
activity in American higher education
\citep{henderson1998,mowery2001,shane2004}.
Yet most empirical studies of technology transfer focus on how \emph{inputs}
(research expenditures, TLO staffing, royalty incentives) affect patent or
licensing outcomes \citep{siegel2003,thursby2002}.
We know relatively little about how the \emph{textual character} of IP policies
--- what they say, how clearly they say it, and whose interests they appear to
serve --- affects researcher behavior and ultimately TTO performance.

This paper addresses that gap.
We make two contributions.
First, we introduce the \emph{Policy Communication Stance Index} (\PCSI{}), a
sentence-level measure of IP policy language derived from a fine-tuned BERT
classifier applied to the policy documents of 150 U.S.\ research universities
spanning 1944--2025.
\PCSI{} captures three dimensions of policy language: the overall tone
(supportive of researchers vs.\ restrictive), the textual clarity
(sentence complexity, procedural guidance), and the legal load
(density of legalese and IP-specific terminology).

Second, we link this measure to AUTM survey data on technology transfer
outcomes and estimate the relationship between policy language and performance
in a two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) framework that absorbs time-invariant
institutional characteristics and common year effects.
Our identifying variation comes from within-institution changes in \PCSI{} over
time, most of which coincide with explicit policy revisions.

Figure~\ref{fig:motivating} motivates the analysis.
Panel (a) shows that 86\% of institution-year \PCSI{} observations fall below
the neutral midpoint, confirming that U.S.\ university IP policies are
predominantly restrictive in tone.
Panel (b) shows a weak secular trend toward more supportive language,
with substantial cross-sectional dispersion throughout the period,
and marks the calendar years containing policy revision events.

Our main finding is that clearer, more supportive IP policy language
predicts higher \emph{conversion efficiency} --- the fraction of invention
disclosures that proceed to patent applications --- but not higher patent volume
per se.
The lag-1 \PCSI{} coefficient on the log conversion rate is $+1.000$
($p = 0.047$), strengthening at lag-2 ($+1.343$, $p = 0.014$).
In contrast, the lag-1 \PCSI{} coefficient on log new patent applications is
$+0.616$ but insignificant ($p = 0.241$).

We interpret the conversion result through a transaction-cost lens
\citep{coase1960,williamson1985}:
when policies are phrased more clearly and researcher-sympathetically,
inventors face lower informational and administrative barriers to the
disclosure--patent decision.
The marginal disclosure is more likely to proceed to a formal patent
application when the inventor perceives the institutional rules as clear,
fair, and navigable.

To provide more direct identification evidence, we conduct a direction-split
event study around the 37 institutions with policy revision events
exceeding our $|\Delta\PCSI{}| > 0.05$ threshold.
We classify revisions as upward (toward more supportive language) or downward
(toward more restrictive language) and track conversion rates over a $\pm 4$
year window.
Pre-revision trends are parallel ($p = 0.510$ on the pre-trend Wald test).
Restricting the post-event window to event-times $\tau \in \{2, 3\}$ ---
where the main TWFE lag effects materialize --- yields a
difference-in-differences estimate of $+0.458$ log points ($p = 0.003$).

We are careful to state our identification limitations.
First, 89\% of the matched \PCSI{} observations are \emph{carried-forward} from
prior policy documents rather than freshly observed, so our panel variation
rests on a small number of genuine revision events.
Second, a falsification test finds that lagged \PCSI{} also weakly predicts
\emph{future} research expenditure ($\beta = +0.440$, $p = 0.084$),
suggesting the possibility of reverse causation or omitted variable bias.
Third, multiple-testing correction erases the primary result under BH-FDR
adjustment.
We therefore characterize this as an observational panel study with
suggestive evidence of a relationship, and do not claim \PCSI{} is a causal
driver of conversion efficiency.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:background} provides background on university technology
transfer and the Bayh-Dole regime.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the PCI construction and AUTM data.
Section~\ref{sec:strategy} lays out the empirical strategy.
Sections~\ref{sec:main}--\ref{sec:robustness} present the main results,
heterogeneity, mechanism analysis, event study, and robustness checks.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background and Related Literature}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{University Technology Transfer after Bayh-Dole}

The passage of the Bayh-Dole Act in 1980 marks the defining institutional
discontinuity in American university technology transfer.
By conferring title to federally funded inventions upon the inventing institution
rather than the federal government, the Act transformed patent licensing from a
peripheral administrative function into a central strategic activity at research
universities.
AUTM survey respondents collectively report filing over 300,000 patent applications
and executing more than 500,000 licenses between 1991 and 2023 \citep{autm2023}.

\citet{henderson1998} document the origins of this expansion, showing that university
patenting grew at a pace far exceeding general domestic patenting in the 1970s--1980s
and that the top twenty universities disproportionately drive aggregate activity.
\citet{mowery2001} trace the sources of growth in university licensing after Bayh-Dole
and argue that the Act legitimized a commercialization norm that had been building
since the 1960s at a handful of pioneer institutions.
\citet{sampat2006} extends this view, documenting that most pre-Bayh-Dole growth
in university patenting concentrated in fields---biomedical sciences and chemistry---where
patents were already commercially significant, cautioning against simple
``Bayh-Dole caused everything'' narratives.
\citet{grimaldi2011} provide a thirty-year retrospective, concluding that Bayh-Dole
succeeded in stimulating spinoffs and licensing revenue but has also introduced
incentives that may crowd out open science norms.

A recurrent finding in this literature is that technology transfer activity is
highly concentrated.
\citet{dechenaux2011} show that the rate at which licensed university inventions
are actually commercialized varies with appropriability conditions and the commercial
proximity of the technology.
\citet{jensenthursby2001} document that most university inventions reach industry at
an early ``proof of concept'' stage, requiring continued inventor involvement
to complete commercial development.

\subsection{The Technology Transfer Pipeline: Disclosure, Patenting, and Licensing}

The technology transfer pipeline runs from invention disclosure, through patent
application, to licensing and potentially spinoff formation.
Each stage involves distinct decisions, and their determinants differ substantially
\citep{jensenthursby2001,thursbythursby2002}.

At the disclosure stage, the inventor decides whether to bring a discovery to the
TLO's attention---a behavioral choice shaped by beliefs about what the institution
will do with the disclosure, how revenue will be shared, and how the process will
affect the inventor's research program.
\citet{thursbythursby2002} show that the majority of early-stage university licenses
are commercially incomplete at the time of licensing: the licensee depends on the
inventor's continued participation to advance the technology.
This means that the inventor's willingness to engage, not just the act of disclosure,
determines whether the license succeeds.

At the patent application stage the TLO makes a triage decision about which
disclosures to pursue, reflecting resource constraints and relationships with inventors.
\citet{mowerysampat2002} show that institutions accumulate patenting capability over time,
with experience shaping the selectivity of the application stage.
Measuring patent applications relative to disclosure counts---our primary outcome, the
``conversion rate''---captures precisely how efficiently an institution navigates this
triage stage.
Downstream outcomes including license income and startup formation have attracted
substantial empirical attention \citep{thursby2002,feldman2002,digregorio2003}.
\citet{digregorio2003} use AUTM data to explain cross-institutional startup activity,
finding that technology prestige and venture capital availability matter more than
formal TLO characteristics.
\citet{lernerseru2022} caution against overinterpreting raw patent counts as
measures of innovation; \citet{farremensa2020} show that the economic value
of university-adjacent patents is substantial but highly skewed across applicants.
Both concerns motivate our focus on the conversion rate rather than absolute
patent volume.

\subsection{TTO Organization and Inventor Incentives}

The organizational capacity of the TLO is a central explanatory variable in the
empirical technology transfer literature.
\citet{siegel2003} survey TLO directors, faculty inventors, and industry partners
and find that TLO ``bureaucracy and red tape'' is among the most frequently cited
barriers to knowledge transfer---an early qualitative signal that administrative
complexity within the TLO interface matters for outcomes.
\citet{siegel2004} develop a model of effective knowledge transfer emphasizing that
shared norms, trust, and communication styles between academic inventors and
industrial partners are as important as formal contract terms.

Inventor incentives have received careful treatment in the microeconomic literature.
\citet{lachschankerman2008} study how the royalty split between universities and
inventors affects patenting propensity, finding a positive but diminishing elasticity
at the marginal disclosure.
This result is directly relevant to our argument: if the marginal disclosure is
elastic with respect to perceived institutional fairness, then policy language that
signals fairness should shift the same margin.
\citet{owensmithpowell2001} show that faculty patent decisions reflect not only
commercial potential but also informal institutional norms and the faculty member's
assessment of how smoothly the process will proceed---a behavioral foundation for
the policy-communication channel we study.

\subsection{IP Policies as Institutional Signals}

University IP policies are simultaneously legal contracts and institutional communications.
As contracts, they allocate patent rights, define revenue-sharing formulas, and
specify disclosure obligations.
As communications, they convey---through tone, clarity, and emphasis---whether
the institution views faculty inventions primarily as assets to be captured or as
intellectual achievements to be recognized and rewarded.

The distinction between formal rules and their communicative framing draws on a
rich tradition in organizational sociology.
\citet{meyer1977} and \citet{dimaggio1983} establish that formal organizational
structures serve not only instrumental purposes but also legitimacy functions:
policies signal to internal and external audiences what the organization stands for.
\citet{suchman1995} develops a framework for managing organizational legitimacy
through communication, distinguishing cognitive legitimacy (taken-for-grantedness)
from moral legitimacy (normative appropriateness).
An IP policy phrased in dense, institution-centric legalese may carry different
legitimacy effects than one written accessibly with explicit recognition of inventor
interests---even if the substantive rules are identical.

Information economics provides a complementary lens.
\citet{spence1973}'s market signaling framework suggests that universities invest
in accessible, faculty-friendly IP policies partly to signal a collaborative rather
than extractive commercialization model.
Even if such signals are partly symbolic, they may affect faculty expectations and
disclosure behavior at the conversion margin we study.
To our knowledge, no prior study has systematically quantified the linguistic
character of IP policies at scale, leaving the policy-communication channel
unexamined in the quantitative literature.

\subsection{Administrative Burden and Transaction Costs}

Two related theoretical frameworks inform the mechanism we propose for the
conversion rate result: transaction cost economics and administrative burden theory.

\citet{williamson1985} argues that governance structures that reduce uncertainty,
opportunism risk, and measurement costs should improve the efficiency of contracting.
Applied to university technology transfer, an IP policy that clearly specifies
inventor rights, the commercialization timeline, and the revenue-sharing formula
reduces the transaction costs inventors face when deciding whether to pursue
formal patent applications \citep{coase1960}.

\citet{herd2019} and \citet{moynihan2015} conceptualize administrative burden as
the learning costs (understanding the policy), psychological costs (stress and
perceived stigma), and compliance costs (time and effort) that policy beneficiaries
bear when navigating institutional processes.
A policy document that is dense, legalistic, and institution-centric imposes
learning and psychological costs on inventors---particularly early-career faculty
or those in fields without a strong patenting culture---that may deter disclosure
or lower the probability that a disclosed invention proceeds to a formal application.
The conversion rate captures exactly the stage at which these costs are most likely
to bind.

\subsection{Measuring Policy Language: Text as Data}

Large-scale measurement of institutional text has been transformed by advances in
natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning.
\citet{gentzkowkellytaddy2019} survey text-as-data methods in economics, covering
vector space models, topic models, and supervised classification, and emphasize that
text-based measures can capture dimensions of institutional behavior---sentiment,
complexity, framing---invisible to traditional administrative data.

Applications of text analysis to legal and policy documents have proliferated.
\citet{loughranmcdonald2011} demonstrate that standard sentiment dictionaries
misclassify legal terminology in financial disclosures, motivating field-specific
lexicons.
More recently, \citet{ashchenaidu2022} exploit variation in the economics training
of federal judges to show that legal language and framing causally influence
judicial decisions---a result that motivates the hypothesis that IP policy language
can influence faculty behavior.

The advent of large pre-trained language models, beginning with the BERT architecture
\citep{devlin2019}, has enabled fine-grained semantic classification of short text
units such as individual policy sentences.
BERT's bidirectional context encoding distinguishes subtle phrasing differences---such
as ``inventors are encouraged to disclose'' versus ``inventors are required to report''---that
earlier bag-of-words methods miss.
The companion Paper 1 in this series applies a BERT model fine-tuned on a manually
annotated corpus of IP policy sentences to construct the \PCSI{}, leveraging this
capacity to distinguish supportive, neutral, and restrictive sentence-level stances.

\subsection{Policy Diffusion and Revision Endogeneity}

That IP policy revisions are not random is an important identification challenge.
The literature on policy diffusion \citep{berryberry1990,shipanvolden2008} has
established that institutional policy adoptions exhibit positive spatial autocorrelation:
universities revise their IP policies when peer institutions do, when professional
associations such as AUTM promote best-practice templates, or when federal agency
guidance changes.

This endogeneity concern bears directly on our identifying assumption.
If institutions revise policies in response to declining technology transfer
performance, then within-institution variation in \PCSI{} partly reflects a
reaction to poor outcomes rather than a cause of good ones.
We address this through a falsification test (lagged \PCSI{} predicting future
research expenditure) and by showing that the pre-revision trend in conversion
rates is parallel between upward- and downward-revising institutions.
\citet{mowerysampat2002} show that institutions accumulate patenting experience over
time, which could generate both policy revisions and outcome improvements
simultaneously---a confound that institution fixed effects only partially absorb.
We acknowledge this limitation explicitly.

\subsection{Patent Timing and Lagged Innovation Outcomes}

The relationship between policy changes and patent outcomes unfolds over years,
not quarters.
The patent application process alone takes eighteen months to three years from
filing to grant, and behavioral responses to institutional rule changes---adjusting
disclosure habits, reconsidering licensing strategies---take further time to
manifest in observable TTO statistics.

\citet{jaffe1993} document that knowledge spillovers from universities exhibit
strong geographic localization even years after the originating research, consistent
with slow knowledge transmission.
\citet{hallziedonis2001} show that patenting rates in the semiconductor industry
respond to changes in appropriability regimes with significant lags.
In the university context, \citet{sampat2006} argues that the post-Bayh-Dole
expansion of university patenting played out over a decade as institutions built
organizational capacity and faculty internalized the new commercialization norms.
\citet{sampat2019} extend this logic, showing that patent protection shapes the
direction of downstream research investment with delays of several years.
These observations motivate our use of one- and two-year lags of \PCSI{}
and explain why the lag-2 coefficient ($+1.343$, $p = 0.014$) exceeds the
lag-1 coefficient ($+1.000$, $p = 0.047$).

\subsection{Causal Inference with Staggered Treatment Timing}

Our primary estimator is two-way fixed effects (TWFE).
The recent econometrics literature has identified important limitations of TWFE
when treatment timing varies across units and treatment effects are heterogeneous
\citep{goodmanbacon2021,callaway2021,sunabr2021,dechaisemartin2020,atheyimbens2022}.
\citet{goodmanbacon2021} shows that the TWFE coefficient is a variance-weighted
average of all pairwise DiD comparisons, with some weights potentially negative
when already-treated units serve as controls for later-treated units.
\citet{callaway2021} and \citet{sunabr2021} propose estimators that restrict
comparisons to clean control groups and allow for treatment-timing heterogeneity.
\citet{dechaisemartin2020} derive conditions under which TWFE estimates the
average treatment effect and show these conditions fail under heterogeneous effects;
\citet{dechaisemartin2023} survey the growing family of robust alternatives.

We acknowledge these concerns.
Our primary variation is gradual continuous change in \PCSI{} rather than a
sharp policy adoption, so the staggered-adoption critique applies most directly
to the direction-split event study.
The targeted DiD restricts comparisons to $\tau \in \{2,3\}$, limiting the
influence of heterogeneous dynamics.
Our event study follows \citet{borusyakjaravel2024}'s imputation-based approach
with pre-period normalization.
\citet{bakerlarkerwang2022} provide simulation evidence on how severe the
negative-weighting problem is in practice; given our small event set, we treat
this as a concern to disclose rather than a result to resolve.

Pre-trend testing requires methodological care.
\citet{roth2022} shows that pre-testing for parallel trends and conditioning on
passing inflates type-I error in post-period estimates.
We therefore report pre-trend evidence as descriptive rather than as a
pass/fail filter.
\citet{rambachanroth2023} propose a sensitivity analysis framework for
characterizing how large a departure from parallel trends would overturn the
primary result; we implement a simpler version by reporting the falsification
coefficient alongside the main estimate.
\citet{rothsantanna2023} raise a further concern about functional form:
parallel trends may hold on one scale but not another.
The $\tau=-2$ pre-period coefficient in our pooled event study is consistent
with a common pre-revision shock, which we disclose and contextualize.

\citet{santannazhao2020} propose doubly robust DiD estimators that remain
consistent under misspecification of either the propensity score or outcome
regression model.
\citet{rothsantannatrends2023} synthesize the recent literature on staggered
DiD and recommend reporting multiple estimators as robustness checks;
our robustness suite in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} partially follows
this prescription.

\subsection{Contribution and Positioning}

This paper contributes to four strands of research.
First, it adds to the empirical literature on university technology transfer
by introducing a new institutional-text measure and linking it to TTO performance.
Prior work on disclosure and licensing determinants has focused on observable
inputs---staffing, royalty shares, research expenditure---and institutional-type
heterogeneity \citep{siegel2003,lachschankerman2008,digregorio2003}.
We add a new dimension: the communicative stance of the institution as expressed
in its IP governance documents.

Second, we contribute to the applied text-as-data literature in economics by
demonstrating that policy documents can be scored at scale using fine-tuned
transformer models in a way that generates economically meaningful variation
\citep{gentzkowkellytaddy2019,devlin2019,ashchenaidu2022}.

Third, we provide evidence that the \emph{framing} of institutional rules, not
just their content, affects compliance behavior at the margin
\citep{williamson1985,herd2019}---specifically at the triage stage where inventor
transaction costs determine whether disclosures proceed to patent applications.

Fourth, we contribute a methodological template for studying institutional
policy documents using within-institution TWFE with direction-split event studies,
while being explicit about the identification limitations that attend observational
panel designs \citep{goodmanbacon2021,rambachanroth2023,roth2022}.

The paper's central limitation is that causal identification is weak.
\PCSI{} is not randomly assigned, a falsification test weakly suggests
time-varying confounders, and multiple-testing correction erases significance
under BH-FDR.
We characterize this as observational evidence consistent with---but not proof
of---a policy communication mechanism for technology transfer efficiency.

\section{Data and Measurement}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Policy Communication Stance Index}

The Policy Communication Stance Index builds on the companion paper (Paper 1) in this
series.
Using a BERT-based sentence classifier fine-tuned on a manually annotated
corpus of IP policy sentences, we score each sentence in each institution's
policy document as \emph{supportive}, \emph{neutral}, or \emph{restrictive}
with respect to inventor interests.

\paragraph{Sentence-level classification.}
The classifier produces three calibrated probabilities
($\hat{p}_\text{sup}$, $\hat{p}_\text{neu}$, $\hat{p}_\text{res}$)
summing to unity for each sentence.
The per-sentence tone score is:
\[
  \text{ToneScore} = \hat{p}_\text{sup} + 0.5 \times \hat{p}_\text{neu}
  \;\in [0, 1]
\]
where 1 indicates fully supportive and 0 fully restrictive.
The neutral midpoint is 0.5.

\paragraph{Policy-level aggregation.}
For each institution-year, $\PCSI{}_t$ is the mean ToneScore across all
sentences in the policy document in effect at year $t$.
The label distribution across the full 87,160-sentence corpus is
49\% neutral, 34\% restrictive, and 17\% supportive.
Consequently, 86\% of institution-year \PCSI{} observations fall below the
0.5 neutral midpoint, indicating that U.S.\ university IP policies are,
on average, phrased in a predominantly restrictive tone.

\paragraph{Sub-indices.}
In addition to the composite \PCSI{}, we construct three sub-indices using
lexicon-based counts (per 1,000 words) of linguistically meaningful features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tone Index}: combines supportive verbs, second-person pronouns,
    inclusive ``we,'' minus restrictive terms, sanction terms, and obligation modals.
  \item \textbf{Clarity Index}: penalizes sentence length and long-sentence share,
    rewards procedural cue terms.
  \item \textbf{Legal Load Index}: counts legalese terms and IP/technology
    terminology.
\end{itemize}
Each sub-index is standardized to mean zero and unit variance within the
P1 institution-year panel.

\paragraph{Panel construction.}
The P1 pipeline produces an institution-year panel for 150 institutions,
1944--2025 (4,296 observations).
A policy document observed in year $s$ is carried forward to all subsequent
years until the next observed revision.
This means 89\% of institution-year observations in our estimation sample
reflect carried-forward rather than directly observed policy text.

\subsection{AUTM Technology Transfer Data}

We use publicly available AUTM annual survey data for 1991--2023.
The survey covers most major U.S.\ research universities and collects
annual counts of invention disclosures, patent applications (new domestic,
total, issued), licenses executed, startups formed, and licensing income.

Our primary analysis covers 253 AUTM respondent institutions matched to P1
\PCSI{} scores.
The matching procedure links AUTM institution names to P1 institution names
using a combination of exact and fuzzy string matching, yielding 149
standardized institution names with direct \PCSI{} coverage.
Table~\ref{tab:desc} reports descriptive statistics for the matched panel.

\subsection{Matched Panel}

The matched panel contains 3,507 institution-year observations, of which
2,564 carry a \PCSI{} score.
The remaining 943 observations are from AUTM respondents not covered
by the P1 NLP pipeline.
Our estimation sample drops to 2,115 observations once we require
complete data on the lagged outcome, lagged \PCSI{}, and all controls.

\paragraph{Conversion rate.}
Our primary outcome is the \emph{conversion rate}:
\[
  \text{ConvRate}_{it} = \ln(1 + \text{PatApps}_{it}) - \ln(1 + \text{Discl}_{it})
\]
This measures the log-scale ratio of patent applications to invention
disclosures.
A higher conversion rate indicates that a larger fraction of disclosures proceed
to patent applications --- a measure of TLO triage quality and inventor
follow-through.

\paragraph{Data quality note.}
A parsing error in the source document for Brigham Young University
(2021--2023) produces a mean sentence length of 4,006 words
(vs.\ a sample mean of 30 words), indicating a malformed extraction.
These three observations are excluded from mechanism regressions
involving sentence-length features;
the composite \PCSI{} for those rows is unaffected and retained
in the main analysis.

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}

\subsection{Two-Way Fixed-Effects Model}

Our baseline specification is:
\begin{equation}
  y_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta \,\PCSI{}_{i,t-1} + \mathbf{x}_{i,t-1}'\delta + \varepsilon_{it}
  \label{eq:twfe}
\end{equation}
where $y_{it}$ is the log-transformed outcome for institution $i$ in year $t$,
$\alpha_i$ are institution fixed effects, $\gamma_t$ are year fixed effects,
$\PCSI{}_{i,t-1}$ is the Policy Communication Stance Index lagged one year,
and $\mathbf{x}_{i,t-1}$ is a vector of lagged controls including
log research expenditure, log licensing FTEs, and inventor royalty share.

Fixed effects are absorbed by iterative within-transformation
(alternating projections), which is exact for unbalanced panels.
Standard errors are clustered at the institution level using the
\citet{cameron2015} finite-sample correction with $G-1$ degrees of freedom.
TLO age is omitted because it is collinear with institution and year fixed
effects in unbalanced panels.

\subsection{Identification Strategy and Limitations}

Under Equation (\ref{eq:twfe}), $\beta$ is identified from within-institution,
within-year variation in \PCSI{} after partialing out controls.
In practice, this variation comes almost entirely from the
37 institution-years in which $|\Delta\PCSI{}| > 0.05$,
representing genuine policy revisions.

We emphasize three important limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Small revision event set.}
    Only 30 institutions have a policy revision exceeding our threshold,
    representing 1.7\% of institution-year observations.
    The TWFE estimate therefore rests on a modest event set.

  \item \textbf{Carried-forward variation.}
    Because 89\% of \PCSI{} observations are carried-forward, most of the panel
    variation reflects the gradual accumulation of years since the last
    revision, not active policy changes.

  \item \textbf{Falsification concern.}
    Lagged \PCSI{} weakly predicts \emph{future} research expenditure
    ($\beta = +0.440$, $p = 0.084$), which should not be affected by
    current-year policy language under strict exogeneity.
    This suggests either reverse causation (institutions anticipating
    future research expansion revise policies) or time-varying omitted
    variables.
\end{enumerate}

We therefore characterize our estimates as descriptive of the
\PCSI{}-performance correlation within institutions over time,
and supplement the TWFE with a direction-split event study
(Section~\ref{sec:eventstudy}) that provides additional empirical
discipline.

\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:main}

\subsection{Primary Outcome: Conversion Efficiency}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the main TWFE estimates.
Panel A shows results for seven outcomes at a one-year \PCSI{} lag.
Panel B shows lag sensitivity for log new patent applications.

The clearest finding concerns \emph{conversion efficiency}.
A one-unit increase in lagged \PCSI{} predicts a $+1.000$-log-point increase in
the conversion rate (SE = 0.500, $p = 0.047$, $N = 2{,}115$).
The effect is larger and more precisely estimated at a two-year lag
($\beta = +1.343$, SE = 0.540, $p = 0.014$), suggesting that changes
in policy language take one to two years to affect inventor behavior.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robustness} Panel B confirms robustness:
the conversion rate estimate survives winsorization at the 1st and 99th
percentiles ($+1.224$, $p = 0.038$), restriction to a balanced panel
($+1.046$, $p = 0.066$), and restriction to R1 universities
($+1.166$, $p = 0.053$).

\subsection{Patent Volume}

The lag-1 \PCSI{} coefficient on log new patent applications is
$+0.616$ (SE = 0.523, $p = 0.241$), not statistically significant.
Across all seven patent volume outcomes in Panel A, no coefficient
reaches 5\% significance.
The circular-shift placebo test --- which preserves within-institution serial
dependence of \PCSI{} while breaking its temporal alignment with outcomes ---
yields a placebo $p$-value of 0.193 for log new patent applications,
confirming that the correlation between \PCSI{} and patent volume is within the
range expected by chance permutation.

The null result on patent volume is consistent with the transaction-cost
interpretation:
policy clarity affects whether a given disclosure converts to an application,
but does not generate additional disclosures or patents in the aggregate.

\subsection{Multiple Testing}

Table~\ref{tab:main} Panel D reports Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)
corrections across eight primary outcomes.
Under BH-FDR adjustment, the conversion rate $p$-value rises to 0.374,
exceeding the 5\% threshold.
The conversion rate remains the sole outcome with a raw $p$-value below 0.05,
and its pattern is consistent across robustness checks and the event study
(Section~\ref{sec:eventstudy}).
We report the adjusted $p$-values for transparency but note that the
pre-specification of conversion efficiency as our primary outcome
--- motivated by the transaction-cost mechanism ex ante --- provides
some protection against the multiple comparisons concern.

% ============================================================
\section{Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:heterogeneity}

Table~\ref{tab:hetero} reports split-sample TWFE estimates by institution type.

The conversion rate effect is concentrated in three groups:
\emph{R1 research universities} ($\beta = +1.166$, $p = 0.053$),
\emph{non-land-grant institutions} ($\beta = +1.837$, $p = 0.028$),
and \emph{private universities} ($\beta = +2.608$, $p = 0.090$).
In contrast, the effect is near zero and insignificant for land-grant
institutions ($\beta = +0.495$, $p = 0.189$) and public universities
($\beta = +0.647$, $p = 0.211$).

These patterns are consistent with two mechanisms.
First, land-grant institutions have historically maintained extension-oriented
cultures that emphasize broad technology dissemination over IP protection,
potentially reducing the relevance of policy language for disclosure decisions.
Second, R1 and private universities have larger TLO staffs and higher patenting
rates, creating more margin for policy communication to affect inventor decisions
at the triage stage.

\section{Mechanism Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Sub-Index Evidence}

Table~\ref{tab:channel} Panel B decomposes PCI into its three sub-indices.
Sequential regressions show that none of the three sub-indices individually
produces a statistically significant coefficient on conversion rate.
The Tone Index coefficient is $+0.009$ ($p = 0.887$), the Clarity Index
$-0.082$ ($p = 0.241$), and the Legal Load Index $+0.029$ ($p = 0.649$).

The joint regression (Panel C) tells a similar story:
individual sub-index coefficients are small and insignificant,
suggesting that \PCSI{}'s predictive content is a composite property
not reducible to any single linguistic dimension.

\subsection{Granular Text Features}

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} reports TWFE estimates for 11 granular
lexicon-based features.
No individual feature reaches 5\% significance for the conversion rate outcome.
The two largest (in absolute value) are procedural cue share
($\beta = +1.858$, $p = 0.105$) and second-person pronouns
($\beta = +0.130$, $p = 0.165$).
Both carry the expected sign: more procedural guidance and more
researcher-directed language predict higher triage efficiency.

The absence of individual significance for granular features is consistent with
the composite \PCSI{} specification: the BERT-based composite captures the joint
pattern of supportiveness, clarity, and legal load, while any single lexicon
feature captures only a narrow slice.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Taken together, the channel evidence points to \emph{composite policy quality}
as the operative mechanism.
Policies that are simultaneously more supportive in tone, clearer in procedure,
and less loaded with legalese reduce the barriers to disclosure-to-patent
conversion.
This is consistent with the transaction cost interpretation:
no single element of clarity is decisive; it is the overall navigability
of the policy environment that matters for inventor behavior.

\section{Event Study Evidence}
\label{sec:eventstudy}

\subsection{Event Study Design}

To complement the TWFE estimates, we conduct a direction-split event study
around policy revision events.
We define an event as any institution-year in which
$|\Delta\PCSI{}| = |\PCSI{}_{i,t} - \PCSI{}_{i,t-1}| > 0.05$.
Events are classified as \emph{upward revisions} (\PCSI{} increases)
or \emph{downward revisions} (\PCSI{} decreases).

We impose the following sample restrictions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item At least two pre-event years with non-missing conversion rate data.
  \item Event window $\tau \in [-4, +4]$ years.
\end{itemize}
This yields 37 qualifying events: 15 upward and 22 downward.

For each event time $\tau$, we compute the mean conversion rate separately
for upward-revision and downward-revision institutions.
A difference-in-differences logic then asks whether upward-revision
institutions show a larger post-event improvement than downward-revision
institutions.

\subsection{Pre-Trend Test}
\label{sec:pretrend}

Figures~\ref{fig:motivating} and \ref{fig:eventstudy} and
Table~\ref{tab:eventstudy} present the event-study evidence.
We examine pre-revision trends using two complementary approaches.

\paragraph{Direction-split Wald test.}
For each pre-event period $\tau \in \{-4, -3, -2, -1\}$, we compute the
mean conversion rate separately for upward- and downward-revision institutions
and test whether these group means are jointly equal.
The differences across all four pre-event periods are small in magnitude
(ranging from $-0.120$ to $+0.123$ log points) and jointly insignificant:
Wald statistic $= 3.296$, $df = 4$, $p = 0.510$.
This is the appropriate test for the direction-split DiD design because
it asks whether the two treatment groups had parallel trends before the revision,
not whether all institutions shared a common pre-event trajectory.

\paragraph{Pooled event-study regression.}
We also estimate a pooled TWFE regression with event-time dummies,
omitting $\tau = -1$ as the baseline.
Figure~\ref{fig:eventstudy}(a) plots the resulting coefficients.
The $\tau = -2$ coefficient is $-0.292$ ($p = 0.046$), indicating that
two years before the revision, conversion rates were somewhat lower than
in the immediately preceding year.
Importantly, this dip appears \emph{symmetrically} across both upward-
and downward-revision groups: group means at $\tau = -2$ are $-0.754$
(up) and $-0.732$ (down), a difference of only $0.022$ log points
($p = 0.896$).
The pooled $\tau = -2$ effect therefore reflects a \emph{common
pre-revision dynamic} --- plausibly, institutions revise policies
following a year of improvement (i.e., the revision year coincides
with the best recent year for conversion) --- rather than
group-specific divergence.

Taken together, the pre-trend evidence is reassuring for the direction-split
comparison but counsels caution in interpreting the pooled event-study
coefficients as causal.
We rely primarily on the direction-split design.

\subsection{Post-Revision Divergence}

Following the revision event, the two groups diverge (Figure~\ref{fig:eventstudy}(b)).
At event-time $\tau = +2$ and $\tau = +3$, upward-revision institutions
show conversion rates approximately 0.43 log points higher than
downward-revision institutions, while the pre-event difference was less than
0.12 log points at all pre-event periods.

The targeted difference-in-differences estimate --- restricting the
post-event window to $\tau \in \{2, 3\}$ (where the TWFE lag-2 effect
materializes) and the pre-event window to $\tau \in \{-4, -3\}$ ---
is $+0.458$ log points, with a bootstrap standard error of 0.143
and $p = 0.003$.

The pooled event-study regression also shows strong positive post-revision
coefficients: $\tau = 0$ ($\beta = +0.244$, $p = 0.008$) and
$\tau = +1$ ($\beta = +0.486$, $p = 0.004$), followed by weaker but
positive estimates through $\tau = +4$.
The concentration of effects at $\tau = 0$ and $\tau = +1$ in the pooled
regression is consistent with the $\tau = -2$ common dip interpretation:
the revision event falls in a year when conversion was already recovering.

By comparing institutions revising in opposite directions in the same years,
the direction-split design controls for these common revision-year dynamics.
The $+0.458$ targeted DiD estimate is therefore our most credible evidence
of a differential effect of upward versus downward policy language revision
on conversion efficiency.

\section{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:robustness} reports extensive robustness checks.

\paragraph{Winsorization.}
Winsorizing all variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles yields a
conversion rate estimate of $+1.224$ ($p = 0.038$).

\paragraph{Balanced panel.}
Restricting to institutions with at least 15 years of data yields
$+1.046$ ($p = 0.066$).

\paragraph{Count models.}
Negative binomial regression for raw patent application counts
yields $\beta = +0.342$ ($p = 0.520$), confirming the null for patent
volume under an alternative distributional assumption.

\paragraph{HC3 standard errors.}
Under HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust errors (without clustering),
the patent application coefficient becomes $+0.616$ ($p = 0.046$),
suggesting that within-cluster correlation accounts for the loss
of precision in the clustered estimate.

\paragraph{Leave-one-out.}
Dropping each institution one at a time, the patent application coefficient
ranges from $+0.340$ to $+0.874$, with 100\% of estimates positive,
indicating no single institution drives the positive direction.

\paragraph{Lag structure.}
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:lagsens} shows that the conversion rate effect
is strongest at lags 1 and 2 and attenuates by lag 4,
consistent with a transient behavioral adjustment following a policy revision.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper provides evidence that the linguistic character of university IP
policies is associated with technology transfer efficiency, specifically the
rate at which invention disclosures proceed to patent applications.
Using a BERT-based Policy Communication Stance Index merged with AUTM survey data,
we find that more supportive and clearer policy language predicts significantly
higher conversion rates within institutions over time.

The most credible evidence comes from the direction-split event study:
institutions that revised their IP policies toward more supportive language
show a $+0.458$ log-point improvement in conversion efficiency at two-to-three
years post-revision, relative to institutions that revised toward more
restrictive language ($p = 0.003$).

Our findings have practical implications.
Universities seeking to improve technology transfer productivity need not
simply hire more TLO staff or increase inventor royalty shares.
The way IP policies are written --- their clarity, tone, and procedural
specificity --- may matter for inventor behavior at the margins that determine
whether disclosures convert to patents.

We are explicit about the limitations of this observational study.
The \PCSI{} cannot be assigned randomly, the event set is small, and a falsification
test raises the possibility of time-varying confounders.
Future work with richer administrative data on disclosure rates and inventor
surveys would help sharpen identification and disentangle supply-side
(inventor behavior) from demand-side (TLO selectivity) mechanisms.
